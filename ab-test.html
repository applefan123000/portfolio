Reading the results

Three things that decide whether a "significant" result deserves to be shipped.

What the p-value actually says

The p-value is the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if the two variants truly performed identically. Below α (0.05 by default), that explanation becomes implausible — it is not the probability that B is better, and it says nothing about whether the lift is worth having.

Don't peek, don't stop early

Checking significance every day and stopping the moment it dips below 0.05 inflates false positives well beyond 5%. Fix the sample size with the planner before launch, then read the result once — or use a sequential-testing method designed for peeking.

Check the interval, not just the verdict

With enough traffic, a lift of 0.1% can be "significant" and still not worth the engineering cost. If the whole confidence interval clears your minimum detectable effect, the data support a statistically significant and practically meaningful improvement. Also sanity-check the traffic split: a large imbalance you didn't design usually means broken randomization.

Designed & built by Fan (David) Xu

Method: two-proportion z-test with a pooled standard error for the p-value; the confidence interval uses the unpooled standard error (Wald interval, normal approximation). The normal CDF Φ(z) is a test statistic transformation, not the probability that B is better. When either variant has fewer than 100 conversions, the p-value instead comes from Fisher's exact test, which stays accurate at small counts, and the results label which test was used. Every analysis also runs a sample-ratio-mismatch check: a chi-square goodness-of-fit test of the observed visitor split against the expected 50/50 split, flagged when significant at the 1% level.
